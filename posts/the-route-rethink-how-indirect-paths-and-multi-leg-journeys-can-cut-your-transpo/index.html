The Route Rethink: How Indirect Paths and Multi-Leg Journeys Can Cut Your Transport Costs

2026-07-09 · Transport · Tags: budgettravelpro, transport, The

When we plan a trip, our instinct is to seek the fastest, most direct route from point A to point B. We assume that a nonstop flight or an express train is not only the most convenient but also the most economical. But seasoned budget travelers know a counterintuitive truth: the shortest line on the map is often the longest line on your bank statement. By embracing indirect routes, multi-leg journeys, and creative combinations of transport modes, you can slash your travel costs dramatically—and often turn a mundane transfer into an unexpected mini-adventure. This is the art of the route rethink, and it’s one of the most powerful tools in the budget traveler’s arsenal.

The Myth of the Direct Route

Airlines and rail companies price direct services at a premium because they know travelers value time. The convenience of a nonstop flight or a direct train is baked into the ticket price. For example, a nonstop flight from New York to London might cost $600, while a flight with a layover in Dublin could be $350. Similarly, a direct high-speed train from Paris to Barcelona might set you back €120, but taking a slower regional train to Lyon and then connecting to a bus could cost under €50. The difference isn’t just about competition—it’s about the willingness to pay for simplicity. When you let go of the need for a straight line, you unlock a world of savings.

Moreover, direct routes often funnel through major hubs, which are themselves expensive. Airports like Heathrow, JFK, or Frankfurt charge higher fees to airlines, which are passed on to you. By choosing secondary airports or less popular routes, you can bypass these hidden costs. The route rethink starts with a simple mindset shift: instead of asking “How do I get from A to B fastest?” ask “What’s the cheapest way to get from A to B, and what can I see along the way?”

The Flight Layover Loophole

Layovers are often seen as a nuisance, but they can be a goldmine for budget travelers. Many budget airlines and even major carriers offer significantly lower fares on flights with longer layovers. Sometimes, you can even choose a layover that gives you enough time to leave the airport and explore a city for a few hours—without paying extra for a separate trip. For instance, a flight from Bangkok to London might have a 12-hour layover in Dubai. Instead of sitting in the terminal, you can take the metro into the city, visit the Burj Khalifa observation deck (if your budget allows) or simply wander the souks, and still make your connection. The key is to research visa requirements and airport proximity before you book. Some airports, like Singapore Changi, even offer free city tours for transit passengers.

But the layover loophole isn’t just about long stops. Even a short layover can save you money if you book two separate tickets instead of a single connection. This is known as “self-connecting.” For example, flying from Los Angeles to Reykjavik on one ticket might cost $800, but booking a separate flight from LA to New York for $150 and then a budget carrier from New York to Reykjavik for $200 could total $350. The risk is that if your first flight is delayed, you might miss the second, but with careful scheduling (leaving a generous buffer) and travel insurance, this can be a massive money-saver. Always compare the total cost of a self-connect against a through-ticket—and remember to factor in baggage fees if you need to recheck your luggage.

The Train vs. Bus Trade-Off

On the ground, the train vs. bus debate is a classic budget dilemma. Trains are often faster, more comfortable, and more scenic, but they come with a premium price tag. Buses, on the other hand, are the undisputed champions of the budget traveler. A bus from Rome to Florence might take 3 hours and cost €15, while a train takes 1.5 hours and costs €50. If you have time to spare, the bus is a no-brainer. But the route rethink goes deeper: you don’t have to choose one mode for the entire journey. You can mix and match. Take a train for the long-haul leg where speed matters, then switch to a bus or local minibus for the final stretch. For example, traveling from Berlin to Prague: a direct bus might take 4.5 hours and cost €25, while a train takes 4 hours and costs €40. But you could take a regional train from Berlin to Dresden (2 hours, €20) and then a local bus from Dresden to Prague (2.5 hours, €10). That’s a total of €30, and you get to see Dresden along the way. The extra hour is worth the €10 savings, and you’ve turned a boring transfer into a mini-city break.

Another trick is to use overnight buses instead of trains. Overnight buses often cost half the price of a sleeper train, and you save a night’s accommodation. Many budget bus companies like FlixBus, Megabus, and local operators offer reclining seats, Wi-Fi, and even power outlets. While not as luxurious as a train cabin, they’re perfectly fine for a night’s sleep. The route rethink here is to consider the total cost: a bus ticket plus a night in a hostel might be more expensive than an overnight train with a bed. But if you’re comfortable sleeping upright, the bus is unbeatable. Always compare the full cost—including accommodation savings—before you book.

The Multi-Leg Strategy for Flights

When it comes to flying, breaking your journey into multiple legs can yield enormous savings, especially if you’re flexible about your departure and arrival airports. This is the essence of the route rethink: instead of booking a single round-trip ticket, you book separate one-way flights, or even use different airlines for different segments. For example, a round-trip from Chicago to Lisbon might cost $900. But you could book a one-way from Chicago to Dublin on a budget airline for $300, then a separate one-way from Dublin to Lisbon on a regional carrier for $100. That’s $400 for the outbound, and if you do the same on the return, you’re at $800—still a savings, and you’ve also seen Dublin. The trick is to use flight search engines that allow multi-city searches, like Google Flights or Skyscanner, and to be open to nearby airports. Flying into a smaller airport like London Luton instead of Heathrow, or Paris Beauvais instead of Charles de Gaulle, can save you hundreds, even after factoring in the cost of a bus or train into the city center.

Another multi-leg strategy is the “hidden city” trick, where you book a flight with a layover in your intended destination and simply get off there, skipping the final leg. For instance, if you want to fly from New York to London, you might find a cheaper flight from New York to Paris with a layover in London. You book the whole ticket, but you only use the first leg to London, and you don’t board the second leg. This is controversial and can lead to airline penalties if you do it frequently, but for a one-off trip, it can save a lot. However, it’s risky—if your first leg is delayed, you might miss the connection, and you can’t check bags through. Use it sparingly and only when you’re confident. The safer multi-leg approach is to simply book separate tickets with a long layover, as mentioned earlier.

The Rideshare and Carpooling Option

Beyond trains and buses, ridesharing platforms like BlaBlaCar have revolutionized intercity travel in Europe and beyond. These apps connect drivers with empty seats to passengers heading the same way. You pay a small fee to cover fuel costs, and you get a direct, door-to-door ride that’s often cheaper than a bus or train. For example, a ride from Madrid to Valencia might cost €15 on BlaBlaCar, while a bus costs €25 and a train €45. The journey is faster because you don’t stop at multiple stations, and you get to meet locals. The route rethink here is to consider ridesharing for medium distances—anywhere from 100 to 500 kilometers. It’s not always available, but in countries with a strong car culture, it’s a fantastic budget option. Always check the driver’s ratings, communicate in advance, and agree on pickup points. Safety first, but the savings and social aspect are unbeatable.

Another rideshare variant is long-distance carpooling for overnight trips. Some drivers are happy to split the cost of a long journey, and you can take turns driving or sleeping. This is more common in the US and Australia, but it’s growing globally. Websites like Craigslist or local Facebook groups can be goldmines. Just be cautious and meet in public places. The key is to be flexible with your schedule—you’re not on a fixed timetable, but you’re saving money and often making a friend.

The Local Transport Combination

Finally, the route rethink extends to the last mile of your journey. Many budget travelers make the mistake of taking a taxi from the airport or train station to their accommodation, which can eat up a huge chunk of their daily budget. Instead, combine local transport modes: a public bus from the airport to the city center, then a metro or tram to your neighborhood, and finally a short walk. In many cities, this costs under $5, compared to a $30 taxi. But the real hack is to use minibuses, shared taxis, or “matatus” in Africa, “songthaews” in Thailand, or “jeepneys” in the Philippines. These are local, shared vehicles that follow fixed routes and are incredibly cheap. They might not be comfortable, but they’re authentic and often the fastest way to get around. For example, in Vietnam, you can take a local bus from Ho Chi Minh City to the Mekong Delta for a few dollars, while a private car would cost $50. The route rethink means being willing to ask locals, read signs, and hop on a vehicle that doesn’t have a set schedule—it leaves when full. This adds adventure and saves money.

Even within a city, combining walking with public transit can cut costs. Instead of taking a bus for a 20-minute walk, walk it. Not only do you save money, but you also see more. The route rethink is about being intentional with every transport choice. Before you book any ticket, ask yourself: Is there a cheaper, slightly longer way? Can I break this journey into parts? Can I use a local mode instead of a tourist one? The answers will lead you to significant savings.

Conclusion

The direct route is a luxury, not a necessity. By rethinking your travel routes, you can turn a simple transfer into a money-saving adventure. Whether it’s embracing layovers, mixing buses and trains, booking separate flight legs, hopping into a rideshare, or navigating local minibuses, the route rethink empowers you to take control of your transport budget. The next time you plan a trip, don’t just search for the fastest way—search for the smartest way. Your wallet will thank you, and you’ll return home with stories that no direct flight could ever offer.
